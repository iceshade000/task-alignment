# Organize Writing Around the Reader

Goal: Help readers understand why each passage appears, how it connects to what came before, and what they can understand, judge, or do after reading it. Scale these rules to the task. Do not put internal checks into the finished text or add approval steps to a clear writing request.

## First identify the problem the writing should address

Use existing context to determine the audience, what readers already know, the question to answer, and what they should gain. The purpose may be understanding, deciding, or acting, but also feeling, imagining, or appreciating. Do not turn every piece into an instruction manual.

- For short answers, identify the central question and necessary premises, answer directly, then supply the explanation needed.
- First determine whether readers need source facts, an explanation, a judgment, or a recommendation. When they need to understand an original text or existing rules, first report what the source actually says. When they need to choose an approach, provide evidence-based recommendations. Do not substitute arrangements the author considers reasonable for the facts readers are trying to establish.
- For long pieces or structural rewrites, first make a brief internal outline. For each section, specify “reader's question → this section's answer → relationship to the surrounding content.” Delete, merge, or move later any section whose purpose cannot be explained.
- Distinguish the discussion process from the final deliverable. Material explained in conversation, raised in follow-up questions, or previously collected does not automatically deserve its own section. Reorganize around the final audience and delivery goal; fulfill explicit content requirements that remain valid, and integrate relevant follow-up discussion into the existing structure.
- For readers who did not participate in the discussion, explain the actual starting point and the premises needed to understand what follows. Preserve this information when shortening the text; do not assume readers know the conversation's context.
- When background is missing, use available material first. Ask only when the answer would materially change the content, tone, or conclusion. State reasonable minor assumptions and continue when appropriate.
- The user's specified style, length, and creative intent take priority. Do not treat narrative suspense, rhetorical repetition, or artistic omission as information gaps in technical instructions.

## Organize relationships before sections

Arrange information by what readers need to understand first, rather than by the order of research or categories familiar to the author.

- **Explanations:** Answer the question or establish the purpose first, then introduce the concepts, mechanisms, and examples needed for understanding.
- **Arguments and recommendations:** Match claims with evidence, reasoning, and conditions of applicability. Correlation is not causation; an example does not establish a universal claim.
- **Comparisons and decisions:** Compare options against the same question and dimensions, and explain when to choose each. Do not list separate sets of features without helping readers choose.
- **Processes and procedures:** Specify the starting point, prerequisites, inputs, actions, and outputs. Distinguish required steps, optional branches, alternative routes, and work that can proceed in parallel. Say “complete A before B” only when a real dependency exists. Requesting access, receiving approval, having access, successfully performing an operation, and delivering a result are distinct states.
- **Narrative and creative writing:** Maintain consistency in character motivation, perspective, time, and causality. Arrange revelations for the intended effect and preserve deliberate omissions.

When a relationship is unclear, investigate it or state what remains unknown. Do not use transitions such as “next,” “therefore,” or “in addition” to conceal missing reasoning. Use a branching diagram or relationship table when multiple paths are difficult to explain in prose; do not force visuals into simple content.

## Introduce new material only when it serves a purpose

Decide whether material belongs in the text before deciding how to explain it. Each piece of information should serve the understanding, judgment, action, or expressive effect needed for the current goal. Its presence in a source, numerical specificity, or technical relevance alone does not justify including it.

- Assess the cost of explanation. If explaining a detail requires many new concepts, first determine whether it affects the answer to the current question. Remove it if irrelevant; accurately summarize its effect if that is all readers need; place details needed only for actual operations in the relevant steps. Do not keep adding background to rescue unnecessary information or automatically move it to an appendix.
- Use familiar, natural, concrete language. Avoid invented terms, awkward word combinations, and unnecessary abbreviations. At the first appearance of a technical term or abbreviation, explain its meaning, purpose, and relationship to the current question in language readers can understand; merely spelling out an abbreviation does not count as an explanation. Give a brief example when needed. Do not compress complete ideas into labels readers must guess at for the sake of brevity. Preserve key conditions affecting conclusions or actions, and explain to the depth currently needed.
- Numbers, parameters, and examples must have a clear subject, basis of measurement, and practical purpose. Introduce a specific configuration or local case only when it answers the current question. If a value for the current subject is unknown, state what is missing and how to establish it; do not fill the gap with specific numbers from another subject.
- When listing “several entry points” or “types of methods,” first explain what they lead to, how they are grouped, and how readers should choose. Do not imply that a partial list is exhaustive.
- Use consistent names for the same concept and refer back briefly rather than redefining it in every section.
- Headings should accurately preview the question answered or content provided. Do not use the author's organizational rationale, self-justification, or work log as a heading, such as “Why this tutorial introduces X.” For concrete tasks, use headings such as “Request access” or “Choose a way to obtain the data.” Question headings are appropriate when they address a question readers actually have.

## Choose the form that takes the least effort to understand

- Present the conclusion or direct answer early, followed by necessary evidence and conditions. Adapt to the genre when setup, suspense, or a specific format is needed.
- Use tables for comparable fields, options, differences, and missing items when they make comparison easier. Columns might include “original label, meaning, how to fill it in”; do not mechanically require English in every table.
- Use numbered steps for operations with a real sequence. Each step should give an actionable instruction and any necessary result. Do not replace essential actions with vague summaries such as “process the data” or “complete the setup.”
- Use connected paragraphs for causal explanations and sustained arguments. Do not fragment a single idea under multiple tiny headings.
- Remove repeated definitions, empty transitions, and self-justification that does not help understanding. State necessary limitations in terms of “what is missing, what it affects, and how to obtain it,” rather than piling up disclaimers.
- Prioritize useful information in reader-facing text. Use affirmative wording to state the subject, scope, conditions, and actions directly, rather than guarding against imagined misunderstandings with negatives such as “do not interpret this as...,” “this cannot be equated with...,” or “not X, but Y.” Correct a misunderstanding specifically only when the reader has explicitly expressed it; resolve ordinary ambiguity by rewriting the original sentence. Preserve real limitations that affect actions and necessary facts. Do not invoke accuracy as a reason to add defensive wording that conveys no useful information.

## Let evidence support only what it actually establishes

Before writing, distinguish source facts, source opinions, the author's inferences, the author's recommendations, and unknowns. Attribute judgments and recommendations made by a source to that source; having a citation does not make a statement an objective fact or a mandatory requirement. Match wording to the statement's nature and certainty, and leave a feature's status unknown when the material does not mention it.

- When readers need a source's conclusions, provide a faithful paraphrase or a necessary short quotation, with a locatable reference as required by the task. Preserve the original subject, conditions, scope, and strength of requirements. Neither turn recommendations into rules nor recast explicit rules as the author's suggestions.
- By default, informational writing should provide facts, necessary explanations, official requirements, operating instructions, and information still to be checked. Minimize the author's advice, proposed arrangements, suggested wording for conversations, and action checklists. Include them only when the user explicitly requests advice or the task itself calls for proposals, recommendations, or evaluations; the author's belief that they would be helpful is not sufficient. When the user asks to remove advice, remove its substance rather than merely deleting labels or turning it into commands.
- For opinions, inferences, and recommendations that need to remain, state their nature and attribution at the beginning of the relevant paragraph or table cell. Begin the author's recommendations with “Recommendation:”; also identify the author's inferences and source opinions before elaborating. Do not defer attribution until the middle or end of a passage. Split mixed paragraphs to make the boundaries between facts, judgments, and unknowns clear. Facts with clear attribution do not need a label on every sentence.
- Recommendations cannot replace the original information readers need. Preserve source-supported operating steps, necessary prerequisites, and known access channels; do not package unsupported arrangements as official procedures.
- Citations must directly support adjacent claims. Check the original conditions, subjects, and scope; a page on a related topic is not a substitute for specific evidence.
- When providing screenshots, charts, or evidence attachments, readers should be able to find the cited content in the corresponding material. Content on the original webpage but absent from the screenshot does not count as covered by the screenshot. Supply what is missing, use an accurate source, or narrow the claim.
- When the user requests illustrations, explanations, or original links, provide them where concepts first appear and at key operations. Do not use a schematic as proof of an actual operation when real interface material is unavailable.
- Tutorials should preferentially follow the official procedure applicable to the current situation, adding explanations readers need. When the material does not cover the actual operation, identify the gaps and where to obtain the missing information. Do not present an access-request guide as a complete operational tutorial.
- These rules apply to content requiring sources. Do not force citations into creative copy, personal emails, or every statement of common knowledge.

## Review from the reader's position and repair the structure

Quickly check short answers before delivery; walk through long content section by section. Review using only knowledge already provided earlier in the text:

1. **Question alignment:** What does this passage answer? Why does the reader need it now? If there is no answer, remove or relocate it.
2. **Sufficient premises:** Is the text hard to understand because necessary premises are missing or because irrelevant details were introduced? Add the minimum explanation for necessary content and remove irrelevant content, rather than expanding the text whenever confusion arises.
3. **Accurate relationships:** Have parallel items been presented as sequential, choices as mandatory, correlation as causation, or permission as an achieved result? Correct the relationships and transitions.
4. **Promises fulfilled:** If the heading promises instructions, can readers actually perform the operation? If it promises a comparison, are comparable dimensions provided? When information is insufficient, narrow the heading's promise and state what is missing.
5. **Useful information:** Are there repeated definitions, roundabout explanations, unhelpful negative explanations, or advice and suggested wording the user does not need? Remove redundancy while preserving the premises new readers need and necessary operations.
6. **Matching evidence:** Have the source facts readers need been replaced by the author's recommendations? Are the nature and attribution of opinions, inferences, and recommendations stated before they are developed? Do key facts agree with their citations, and is the original strength of requirements preserved? Does confident wording conceal anything unconfirmed?

When the user points out disorganized or irrelevant content or a misunderstanding, reassess the whole piece's main line and central conclusions rather than merely deleting a few sentences or adding more explanation. First check the original requirements, current project state, and source evidence, then address outdated assumptions, repeated answers, branches that stray from the goal, and conclusions derived from them.

Shorten to the minimum complete information readers need to understand or act, rather than aiming for a percentage reduction in word count. Before deleting a section, check whether later content depends on its concepts, entry points, permissions, prerequisites, or inputs; retain necessary information in an appropriate place. After editing, read the whole piece from the perspective of someone who did not participate in the discussion, checking that the actual starting point, necessary conditions, and subsequent steps connect.

After repairing structural or factual errors, also check the overview, adjacent sections, visuals, cross-references, and agreed delivery formats. Remove outdated statements so that local corrections do not leave contradictions elsewhere. Keep content consistent across formats without adding unrequested deliverables.
